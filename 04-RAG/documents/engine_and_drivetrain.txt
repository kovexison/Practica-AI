TITLE: Engine and Drivetrain Basics
CATEGORY: Powertrain
AUDIENCE: New technical staff
NOTE: Simplified training material. Figures are illustrative and not official specifications.

WHAT THE DRIVETRAIN DOES

The drivetrain delivers torque from the power source to the wheels. In a combustion vehicle the power
source is an internal combustion engine. In an electric vehicle it is one or more electric motors. In a
hybrid it is both, coordinated by control software.

COMBUSTION ENGINE LAYOUTS

Our training fleet covers three layouts. The inline four cylinder is compact and efficient and is used
in entry and mid-range models. The inline six is longer but exceptionally smooth, because its firing
order naturally cancels primary and secondary vibration, which is why it needs no balancer shafts. The
V8 is used where high output matters more than packaging efficiency.

TURBOCHARGING

A turbocharger uses exhaust gas to spin a turbine, which drives a compressor that forces additional air
into the cylinders. More air allows more fuel, and therefore more power from a given displacement. This
is why a modern turbocharged two litre engine can outperform an older naturally aspirated three litre.

Turbo lag is the delay between the driver requesting power and the turbo delivering boost. Twin-scroll
housings and smaller, lighter turbine wheels reduce it. Some engines use two turbochargers of different
sizes, a small one for low engine speeds and a large one for high engine speeds.

TORQUE AND POWER

These are commonly confused. Torque is rotational force and determines how strongly the vehicle
accelerates at a given moment. Power is the rate at which work is done and determines how the
acceleration is sustained as speed rises. Power is derived from torque and engine speed. An engine can
produce high torque at low revolutions and still be modest in peak power.

Electric motors produce maximum torque from a standstill, because torque depends on current rather than
on airflow through cylinders. This is why electric vehicles feel immediately responsive from rest even
when their headline power figure is unremarkable.

TRANSMISSIONS

The eight speed automatic transmission is standard across most combustion models. It uses a torque
converter to allow the engine to idle while the vehicle is stationary, with a lock-up clutch that closes
at cruising speed to remove slip and improve efficiency.

Most electric vehicles need only a single speed reduction gear. An electric motor produces useful torque
across a very wide speed range, so multiple ratios add cost and weight for little benefit.

ALL WHEEL DRIVE

All wheel drive distributes torque between front and rear axles for traction. In combustion vehicles a
transfer case with a multi-plate clutch varies the split continuously, sending most torque rearward in
normal driving and shifting it forward when slip is detected.

Electric all wheel drive is achieved differently. A separate motor drives each axle and the split is
controlled purely in software, with no mechanical connection between the axles. Response is faster
because current can be changed in milliseconds, far quicker than a clutch pack can react.

HYBRID CONFIGURATIONS

A mild hybrid uses a small electric machine, typically 48 volts, to assist the engine and to enable
smooth stop-start. It cannot drive the vehicle alone.

A plug-in hybrid has a larger battery and can drive on electricity alone for a meaningful distance,
commonly 50 to 90 kilometres, before the engine is required. It is charged from an external supply.
The benefit is realised only if the owner actually charges it; an uncharged plug-in hybrid carries the
weight of both systems while using only one.
