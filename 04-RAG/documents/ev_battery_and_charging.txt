TITLE: Electric Vehicle Battery and Charging Guide
CATEGORY: Electrification
AUDIENCE: Owners and service advisors
NOTE: Simplified training material. Figures are illustrative and not official specifications.

OVERVIEW

The high-voltage battery is the single most expensive component in an electric vehicle. It stores
energy as direct current (DC) and supplies the electric motor through an inverter. Understanding how
the battery is charged, conditioned and protected explains most of the questions owners ask.

BATTERY CAPACITY: GROSS VERSUS NET

Two capacity numbers are quoted for every pack. Gross capacity is the total energy physically present
in the cells. Net capacity, sometimes called usable capacity, is the portion the vehicle will actually
use. A representative pack in our training fleet has a gross capacity of 83.9 kWh and a net usable
capacity of 81.2 kWh.

The difference is deliberate. The vehicle keeps a buffer at the top and bottom of the range because
cells age fastest when held at very high or very low states of charge. Owners frequently believe the
buffer is a defect. It is not. It is the main reason the pack still performs well after many years.

CHARGING MODES

AC charging draws alternating current from a wall box or public post. The onboard charger converts it
to DC. The onboard charger is the bottleneck in AC charging, and a typical rating is 11 kW. A full
charge from near empty on an 11 kW wall box takes approximately 8 hours and 15 minutes, which suits
overnight charging at home.

DC fast charging bypasses the onboard charger and feeds direct current straight to the pack. Peak
rates of 205 kW are achievable under good conditions. Charging from 10 percent to 80 percent takes
roughly 32 minutes at a suitable high-power charger.

WHY CHARGING SLOWS DOWN ABOVE 80 PERCENT

Charging speed is not constant. The curve tapers as the pack fills. Above roughly 80 percent state of
charge the vehicle deliberately reduces current to protect cell chemistry and manage heat. Going from
80 percent to 100 percent can take almost as long as going from 10 percent to 80 percent. For long
journeys, two shorter stops to 80 percent are usually faster than one long stop to 100 percent.

TEMPERATURE AND PRECONDITIONING

Charging speed depends heavily on battery temperature. A cold pack accepts current slowly because
lithium plating risk rises at low temperature. Preconditioning warms the pack while driving so that it
arrives at the charger in its optimal window, usually between 20 and 40 degrees Celsius. When a fast
charging stop is set as a navigation destination, preconditioning starts automatically. In cold weather
this can cut a charging stop from over 50 minutes to close to the quoted 32 minutes.

RANGE AND CONSUMPTION

Quoted range figures come from standardised laboratory cycles and are useful for comparing vehicles,
not for predicting a specific journey. Real consumption is dominated by speed, temperature and terrain.
Aerodynamic drag rises with the square of speed, so sustained motorway driving is the largest single
drain. Cabin heating in winter is the second largest. A heat pump reduces heating energy substantially
compared with a resistive heater.

Regenerative braking recovers energy during deceleration by running the motor as a generator. In
stop-and-start city traffic, regeneration is why an electric vehicle often consumes less per kilometre
in town than on the motorway, which is the opposite of a combustion vehicle.

BATTERY HEALTH OVER TIME

Capacity loss is normal and gradual. The main factors that accelerate it are frequent DC fast charging,
sustained storage at 100 percent state of charge, and exposure to high ambient temperatures. For daily
use, charging to 80 percent is recommended, reserving 100 percent for days when the full range is
genuinely needed.

The high-voltage battery is covered by a separate warranty from the rest of the vehicle. See the
warranty document for the exact terms, including the capacity retention threshold.
