TITLE: Manufacturing and Quality Control
CATEGORY: Production
AUDIENCE: New production staff
NOTE: Simplified training material.

THE MAIN PRODUCTION STAGES

Vehicle production runs through four principal stages. In the press shop, coils of steel and aluminium
are stamped into panels. In the body shop, several hundred panels are joined by welding, riveting and
adhesive bonding into the body in white, largely by robots. In the paint shop, the body is cleaned,
dipped for corrosion protection, then primed, colour coated and lacquered. In final assembly, the
powertrain, interior, glass and electronics are installed.

Battery assembly is a separate line for electric vehicles. Cells are grouped into modules, modules into
a pack, and the pack is sealed, tested for leaks and electrically verified before it reaches the vehicle.

BUILD TO ORDER

Most vehicles are built to a specific customer order rather than to stock. Consecutive vehicles on the
same line commonly differ in engine, colour, trim and equipment. This variety is the central challenge
of the production system: every part must arrive at the right station in the right sequence.

Just in sequence delivery means suppliers deliver components already ordered to match the build
sequence, so seats for a particular vehicle arrive immediately before that vehicle reaches the station.
The benefit is very low inventory. The cost is fragility, since a single sequencing error disrupts the
line.

QUALITY PHILOSOPHY

Quality is built in rather than inspected in. Each station is responsible for the quality of its own
work, and a defect should be prevented rather than detected later.

Poka-yoke, or error proofing, designs tasks so that the wrong action is physically impossible. A
connector that only fits one way, or a tool that will not release until the correct torque is reached,
removes the possibility of that error rather than relying on attention.

Any operator who identifies a serious problem can stop the line. Stopping is expensive, but releasing a
known defect downstream is far more expensive, because correction cost multiplies at every subsequent
stage and is highest once the vehicle has reached a customer.

MEASUREMENT AND TESTING

Body dimensions are verified on coordinate measuring machines and by in-line optical scanning. Torque
values for safety critical fasteners are recorded automatically and stored against the vehicle
identification number, so any joint can be audited years later.

At the end of the line every vehicle passes a functional test covering electrical systems, a rolling
road check, a wheel alignment check, and a water test for sealing. A proportion of vehicles is taken for
a road test.

TRACEABILITY

Each vehicle carries a build record listing part numbers, supplier batches, software versions and
recorded process values. If a supplier reports a defective batch, traceability identifies precisely
which vehicles received parts from it, allowing a narrow and targeted recall rather than a broad one.

CONTINUOUS IMPROVEMENT

Small improvements proposed by the people doing the work accumulate into large gains over time. Warranty
claims and end of line faults are fed back to the responsible stations, closing the loop between what
the customer experiences and how the vehicle is built.
